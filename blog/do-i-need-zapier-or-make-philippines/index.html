Do You Need Zapier or Make, or Can You Do It Inside Messenger?

By Kathy Urbanski Published July 21, 2026 · 8 min read

Start inside the app you already use. Messenger's own free tools handle instant replies, away messages, FAQ answers, and keyword automations, which covers most of what a small business needs. You only need a tool like Zapier or Make when two different apps have to talk to each other, for example pushing every new inquiry into a spreadsheet, or adding a booking to your accounting records automatically. If everything lives in one place, connecting tools solves a problem you do not have.

A lot of small business owners get sold a connection tool before they have anything worth connecting. It is worth understanding the actual dividing line before spending money or time on either. Knowing where that line sits is part of our AI & automation work.

What Messenger can genuinely do on its own

Meta Business Suite's native automation, all free, covers more than most older tutorials admit:

  • Instant replies sent immediately when someone starts a new conversation
  • Away messages sent when your page is offline or outside your set business hours
  • FAQ answers where customers tap a common question and get a pre-written response, with buttons that can link out to your booking page or website
  • Keyword automations that trigger a specific reply when a message contains certain words
  • Comment-to-message in some configurations, sending a DM to someone who comments with a particular keyword

For a page with low to moderate message volume, this is often genuinely enough. There is no separate platform fee for using it. Our auto-reply guide walks through turning it on.

Where native tools stop

The limits are real and worth knowing before you hit them mid-problem:

Character limits. Native auto-replies are commonly capped at around 500 characters, so complex or detailed answers do not fit.

No branching logic. Native tools cannot adapt based on what the customer answers. They cannot ask "what service do you want," then follow a different path for each answer.

No structured data capture. You cannot systematically collect a customer's phone number, budget, and preferred date and have them land somewhere organized. It stays as loose conversation text in your inbox.

No team routing. Multiple staff working the same inbox without a proper shared system means people stepping on each other's replies.

Nothing leaves Messenger. The information stays inside Messenger. It does not reach your spreadsheet, your calendar, or your accounting.

The 24-hour window. Automated messaging outside 24 hours of a customer's last message is restricted under Meta's policy, with narrow exceptions. This limits follow-up regardless of what tool you use.

What Zapier and Make are actually for

Both are connection tools. They sit between apps you already use and move information between them automatically when something happens. A simple example: someone fills in a form on your website. Without a connection tool, you get an email, and you manually copy their details into your spreadsheet, then into your calendar. With one, that happens automatically, every time, without you. Another: a new booking lands in your booking tool, and a row is created in your payment tracking sheet automatically.

The pattern is always the same. Something happens in app A, and something should happen in app B as a result. If you are copying information between two places by hand, repeatedly, that is the signal.

Zapier or Make?

Both do fundamentally the same job. The differences most people encounter:

  • Zapier tends to be more straightforward for simple, linear "when this, then that" automations, with a large library of supported apps
  • Make offers more visual, flexible workflow building with branching and multiple paths, which suits more complex processes but has a steeper initial learning curve

For a first automation, either works. The choice matters far less than whether you actually have a genuine two-app problem to solve.

The honest test for whether you need one

Ask yourself three questions:

  1. Am I regularly copying the same information from one place to another by hand?
  2. Does that copying happen often enough that the time adds up, or does an error in it actually cost me something?
  3. Do both places have a way to connect, meaning they are both real apps rather than a notebook and my memory?

If you answered yes to all three, a connection tool will genuinely help. If you answered no to any, you likely have a different problem: either everything already lives in one place, or the thing you want to automate is not yet in a system that can be automated. That last point catches people out. You cannot automate a process that currently exists only as something you remember to do. It has to live somewhere digital first.

The order that actually works

  1. Turn on free native automation first. Instant replies, FAQs, away messages.
  2. Run it for a month and notice exactly where it falls short. That specific wall is your requirement.
  3. Get your information into real systems, meaning a booking tool, a spreadsheet, a calendar, rather than notebooks and memory.
  4. Then connect them if manual copying between those systems has become a genuine cost.

Most businesses that buy a connection tool at step one end up not using it, because they had nothing to connect yet. It's the same reason we put connection tools last in our first three automations guide.

The learning curve, honestly

Neither tool is difficult, but both take a few hours to become comfortable with, and the first automation you build usually breaks in a small way that takes a while to diagnose. Budget an afternoon, not fifteen minutes. If that afternoon is not available, this is a reasonable thing to have someone set up once, correctly, rather than half-building it yourself over several frustrating weeks.

When the real answer is neither

Sometimes the answer to "should I connect these two apps" is "you should not be using two apps." A business running bookings in one tool, payments in another, customer records in a third, and messaging in a fourth often benefits more from consolidating than from connecting. Fewer moving parts beats better plumbing between many. Working out which situation you are in is genuinely hard from inside the business, because every individual tool was added for a good reason at the time.
