What to Post When You Don't Know What to Post: Content Ideas for Local Businesses

By Daryl Urbanski Published August 31, 2026 · 8 min read

When you're stuck, the fastest path back to a real post is your own recent customer interactions, not a search for creative inspiration. The question a customer asked you yesterday, the thing you fixed this week, the product that just arrived, all of it is content you already lived through and simply have not written down yet. Running out of ideas is almost always a symptom of looking in the wrong place, not a lack of anything worth sharing.

I have sat across from business owners convinced they had nothing interesting to post, while describing, in the very same conversation, three things from that week that would have made genuinely good content. The material exists. It just does not feel like content while it is happening. This is a common blocker we help clear in our social media work.

Why "nothing happened this week" is almost never true

Owners tend to filter out their own daily work as too ordinary to be worth posting, precisely because it is ordinary to them. A customer question you have answered fifty times is brand new information to the fifty-first person who has never asked you. Familiarity with your own work is exactly what makes it hard to see as content.

Ten categories that work when you're stuck

The question you keep answering. Whatever gets asked repeatedly makes a genuinely useful post, and it does double duty by reducing the number of times you have to answer it individually.

Behind the scenes. How something actually gets made, prepared, or done. People are consistently more curious about process than most business owners expect.

A before and after. Visual, immediate, and works across nearly any type of business, a repair, a transformation, a completed project.

A customer story, with permission. A specific situation, not a generic testimonial. "Here's how we helped a customer who needed this by Saturday" reads as real in a way a five-star quote alone does not.

A mistake or a lesson. Something that did not go as planned, and what you learned. This is one of the most underused categories, and it is often the most trusted, because it reads as honest rather than as a sales pitch.

A myth or misconception about what you do. Whatever customers commonly get wrong about your industry or product, corrected clearly.

A small win. A finished project, a compliment received, a milestone. Does not need to be dramatic to be worth sharing.

A local, timely reference. Something happening in your area or season that connects naturally to your business, without forcing it.

A staff or team moment. Puts a human face on the business and builds trust, particularly for service businesses where the person doing the work matters to the customer.

A direct comparison. This versus that, helping a customer make a decision they were already trying to make.

The one-question test for whether an idea is worth posting

Would this be genuinely useful or interesting to the specific person you're trying to reach, or does it only interest you? A post about your business's five-year anniversary matters to you. A post about what that experience means for how a customer gets treated matters to them. Reframe from the reader's side before posting.

Where to actually find these ideas in your week

Rather than trying to invent ideas from nothing, build the habit of noticing them as they happen:

  • Keep a running note on your phone, and add to it the moment something post-worthy happens, not at the end of the week when you have forgotten most of it
  • Review your last month of Messenger conversations for repeated questions
  • Ask your staff what customers have been asking or saying lately, since they often notice things the owner does not

What to do when even this list doesn't help

If a full week genuinely produced nothing across all ten categories, that is itself useful information. It usually means either the week was unusually quiet, worth noting for planning purposes, or that you are filtering too hard, dismissing things as "not interesting enough" that would genuinely interest your actual audience. Sharing an idea with someone else, even briefly, "was this worth a post," often reveals your own filter is stricter than it needs to be.

Repurposing across formats, without more original work

One decent idea can become several pieces of content without requiring fresh material each time: a photo post, then a short video showing the same thing in motion, then a follow-up post answering a question the first one generated. This is not padding. It is legitimately getting more value from something worth sharing, and it connects directly to the batching approach covered elsewhere in this cluster.

The mindset shift that fixes this permanently

Stop trying to think of things to post, and start noticing things worth posting as your week happens. The difference is subtle and it changes everything: the first is invention, which runs out. The second is observation, which does not, because your business keeps generating raw material every single day whether or not you are paying attention to it.
