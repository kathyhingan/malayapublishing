How to Use ChatGPT to Write Customer Replies in Taglish That Don't Sound Robotic

By Kathy Urbanski Published July 26, 2026 · 9 min read

You get natural Taglish replies out of ChatGPT by feeding it several of your own real past messages first, telling it to match that exact tone, then giving it the specific situation. The reason most AI-written replies sound stiff is that people ask for "a professional response" instead of showing the AI how they actually talk. You still read every reply before sending, so you save the typing time without losing the personal voice your customers recognize.

Your customers can tell the difference between a message from you and a message from a template. That difference is often why they chose you over a bigger competitor. The goal here is not to sound like a corporation. It is to stop spending forty minutes a day typing variations of the same three replies. It's a natural companion to the rest of our AI & automation work.

Why most AI replies sound wrong

Ask ChatGPT for "a polite reply to a customer asking about pricing" and you get something technically correct and completely characterless. Worse, when you ask for Tagalog or Taglish, the default output tends toward formal, textbook Tagalog that nobody actually speaks in a Messenger thread. It reads like a government notice. The fix is not a magic prompt phrase. It is giving it examples.

Step 1: Collect your own real messages

Open your Messenger inbox and copy out five to ten of your own actual replies to customers. Pick ones you were happy with. Include the messy ones, the ones with "po," the ones where you switched between English and Tagalog mid-sentence, the ones with your particular way of saying thank you. This is the single highest-leverage step and the one everybody skips.

Step 2: Set up the instruction once

Give ChatGPT a setup message that does three things: establishes who you are, shows your examples, and sets the rules. Something like:

"You help me write customer replies for my [type of business] in [city]. Here are examples of how I actually write to customers: [paste your 5 to 10 real messages]. Match this exact tone. Use natural Taglish the way it appears in my examples, not formal Tagalog. Keep replies short, the length of a Messenger message, not an email. Never invent prices, availability, or promises I have not given you. If you need information I have not provided, ask me instead of guessing."

That last instruction matters enormously. Without it, the AI will happily invent a delivery time or a price to make the reply sound complete, and you will send it without noticing.

Step 3: Ask for the specific situation

Now you can give it short, specific requests:

  • "Customer is asking if we're open on Sunday. We're closed. Offer Saturday instead."
  • "Customer says the order arrived late and they're upset. Apologize, offer to fix it, don't offer a refund yet."
  • "Customer is asking for a discount we don't give. Decline warmly, mention our package deal instead."

Notice how much context you are giving. The quality of what comes back is almost entirely determined by the quality of what goes in.

Step 4: Read it before you send it, every time

This is not optional and it is not a formality. The AI does not know your actual prices, your actual stock, or what you promised this specific customer last week. It will fill gaps confidently and plausibly. A reply that quotes the wrong price or promises next-day delivery you cannot make costs you far more than the two minutes you saved. Read every one. Fix anything that is not true. Then send.

Handling complaints and refund requests

Use extra caution here. AI writes apologies well, sometimes too well, producing something so smooth it reads as insincere, or so accommodating it commits you to a refund you did not intend to offer. For complaints specifically:

  • Ask it for a short reply, not a thorough one. Short reads sincere. Long reads defensive.
  • Explicitly tell it what you are and are not offering
  • Never let it invent an explanation for what went wrong

For anything involving money coming back out of your pocket, write the key sentence yourself and let the AI handle the wrapping around it. The same calm, non-defensive instinct applies to public replies, which we cover in handling a bad Google review.

Where AI gets Taglish wrong

Even with good examples, watch for:

  • Over-formal Tagalog creeping back in over a long conversation. Remind it to check your examples again.
  • "Po" and "opo" used inconsistently, or applied to a customer who is clearly younger, which reads oddly.
  • Regional vocabulary that does not match your area. Filipino is not uniform, and neither is Taglish.
  • Emoji use that is either absent when you would use them or scattered when you would not.

You will catch these easily because you know how you sound. That is exactly why the human read-through stays in the process.

Step 5: Save your best outputs as templates

Once a reply comes out right, save it. Over a few weeks you will accumulate a set of proven templates for your most common situations, and at that point you can often skip the AI entirely for those and just use the saved reply. This is the quiet endgame of doing this well. The goal is not to use AI forever for every message. It is to build a library of replies in your own voice, faster than writing them all from scratch. Those saved replies also feed straight into your Messenger auto-replies.

What this does not solve

AI writing replies does not solve nobody watching the inbox, and it does not solve a business whose real problem is that the answer to most questions is genuinely complicated and specific. If every customer situation is different enough to need real thought, this saves you less than you hope. It works best on the middle band: questions common enough to be repetitive, varied enough that a rigid template feels wrong.
