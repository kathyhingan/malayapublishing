How to Hand Over a Task So It Comes Back Done Right the First Time

A task comes back done right when the handover included four things: what done looks like, when it is due, what decisions the person can make alone, and where to get unstuck. Most work comes back wrong because the instruction covered what to do but not what finished should look like, so the person completed the steps and produced something different from what you pictured.

The result is that quiet, deflating moment where you look at what came back, realize it is not quite it, and do the calculation about whether it is faster to explain or to just fix it yourself. Do that calculation enough times and you stop delegating, which is how owners end up doing everything while employing people. Fixing that is central to the systems work we do with local businesses.

Why "just fix it yourself" is a trap

It is genuinely faster this time. That is what makes it a trap. Each time you absorb the correction instead of closing the gap, three things happen: the person does not learn what right looks like, you confirm to yourself that delegation does not work, and the next handover is slightly more reluctant and therefore slightly worse. The fix is not delegating more. It is delegating with the four elements below, which takes about two extra minutes.

Element 1: What "done" actually looks like

This is the one that is almost always missing. You have a clear picture of the finished thing. The other person does not, and cannot. When you describe only the steps, they follow the steps and produce something that satisfies the steps but not your picture. Describe the output, concretely:

  • Weak: "Post about the new promo."
  • Better: "One Facebook post, up by Friday morning, with the price and the end date, one photo of the product, and the booking link. Same tone as the one we did last month."

That second version takes fifteen extra seconds and removes most of the gap. The strongest version of this: show an example. If a previous version of the work exists that you were happy with, point at it. One example communicates more than a paragraph of description.

Element 2: When it's due, specifically

"When you get a chance" is not a deadline and reliably produces work that arrives after you needed it. Give a real time. If it is genuinely flexible, say so and give an outer limit. If it is part of a chain where someone else is waiting, say that too, because knowing why it matters changes how people prioritize.

Element 3: What they can decide alone

Every task contains small decisions. If you do not say which ones are theirs, one of two things happens: they come back to ask about each one, which recreates the interruption you were trying to avoid, or they guess and you get a different result than expected. Name the boundary explicitly: "Pick whichever photo you think is best, but don't change the price." This single sentence prevents both failure modes.

Element 4: Where to get unstuck

Tell them what to do when something is unclear: who to ask, whether to wait or proceed, and when interrupting you is genuinely fine. A lot of poor work comes from someone who got stuck, felt awkward interrupting, and made an unfortunate call rather than asking. That is a permission problem, not a capability one.

The two-minute handover

Put together, a complete handover sounds like:

"Can you write the Friday promo post? One post, up by Friday 9am, needs the price, the end date, one product photo, and the booking link. Same tone as last month's, take a look at that one. Pick whatever photo works best, but don't change the price or the dates. If anything's unclear, message me before Thursday afternoon."

That is about twenty seconds spoken. It contains all four elements. It is the difference between work that comes back right and work that comes back approximately.

Have them repeat it back, for anything significant

For any task with real consequences, ask them to tell you what they are going to do. Not as a test, framed plainly: "Just so we're on the same page, what's the plan?" Misunderstandings surface here, in ten seconds, rather than at the deadline. This feels slightly awkward the first few times and becomes normal quickly.

Check in at the middle, not the end

For anything taking more than a day, agree a brief checkpoint partway through. A course correction at the halfway point costs a two-minute conversation. The same correction at the deadline costs the whole piece of work, plus the deflation of having it rejected after finishing it. This is also the honest alternative to hovering. A scheduled checkpoint gives you the reassurance that would otherwise leak out as constant checking, which is exactly what a weekly cadence is built to provide.

When it still comes back wrong

Resist the reflex to fix it silently. Instead, work out which of the four elements was missing:

  • They did the wrong thing entirely? "What done looks like" was unclear.
  • Right thing, too late? The deadline was soft.
  • They kept asking you? The decision boundary was undefined.
  • They got stuck and guessed? The unstuck path was not given.

Then give the correction specifically, and add the missing element next time. Two or three rounds of this and the person genuinely learns your standard, at which point handovers get much shorter because the shared picture already exists.

What changes after a few weeks

Handovers get faster, not slower. Once someone has produced work you were happy with three or four times, "same as last time but for the December promo" carries all four elements implicitly. That shorthand is what people mean when they say someone is easy to work with. It is not a personality match. It is accumulated shared context, and it gets built deliberately, one complete handover at a time.

The mindset that makes this hard

Many owners resist this because it feels like the explaining takes longer than the doing. For a one-off task, it might. For anything you will need again, the arithmetic reverses fast, and the tasks worth delegating are almost always the recurring ones. If you find yourself only delegating one-off tasks and keeping all the recurring ones, that is worth noticing. It is exactly backwards from what buys back your time.
