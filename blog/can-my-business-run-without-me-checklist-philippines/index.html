Checklist: Is Your Business Ready to Run Without You for a Week?

Your business can run without you for a week if four things are true: someone else can make the routine decisions, the recurring work is documented well enough to follow, money can move without your personal involvement, and access to every critical account exists outside your phone. Most owner-operated businesses pass on two of these and fail badly on the other two, and the pattern is usually predictable.

This is not really a question about holidays. It is a question about what happens if you get sick, if there is a family emergency, or if you simply need a break before something gives. Most owners find out the answer at the worst possible moment. Answering it deliberately is what our systems work with professional services and local teams is for.

How to use this

Go through each section honestly. Not "could someone figure it out" but "would it actually happen correctly without a call to me." Count your no answers.

Section 1: Decisions

  • Someone can quote a price without asking me
  • Someone can approve a discount within a known range
  • Someone can accept or decline an unusual booking or request
  • Someone can handle a routine complaint to resolution
  • Someone can decide whether to reorder stock or supplies
  • My staff know which decisions are genuinely mine, and it is a short list

If you failed most of this section: this is the highest-value place to start. Decision authority is the bottleneck that costs customers in real time. Our article on the approval bottleneck covers the fix.

Section 2: Knowledge and process

  • The recurring weekly work is written down somewhere findable
  • A person could open that document and follow it without me
  • At least two people can do each critical task
  • The supplier contacts and arrangements are recorded, including verbal ones
  • Customer exceptions and special arrangements are written down, not remembered
  • Someone besides me knows how to handle the thing that always goes wrong

If you failed most of this: start with the two-week interruption log from our "everything is in my head" article. It tells you exactly which knowledge to extract first.

Section 3: Money

  • Payments can be received without me
  • Routine bills can be paid without me
  • Staff can be paid on schedule without my involvement
  • Someone can issue a receipt correctly
  • Daily takings get recorded whether or not I am there
  • Someone would notice if a payment did not arrive

A note on this section: money is where owners are most reluctant to delegate, often for good reason. But "nobody can pay the electricity bill without me" is a different problem from "nobody should be able to move large sums without me," and the two often get conflated. The first is a genuine vulnerability. The second is sensible control.

Section 4: Access

  • Facebook page and Google Business Profile have an owner besides my personal account
  • Someone else can get into the booking system
  • Someone else can access the customer list
  • The physical keys exist in more than one place
  • Critical passwords are somewhere other than my phone
  • If my phone was lost today, the business would still function

If you failed here: this is the fastest section to fix and often the most dangerous to leave. An access audit takes an afternoon. Losing sole access to your own Google Business Profile takes weeks to recover.

Section 5: The human part

  • My staff know who is in charge when I am not there
  • That person knows they are in charge
  • My regular customers have met and been introduced to whoever handles them
  • There is a way to reach me for genuine emergencies, and everyone knows what qualifies
  • My staff would tell me about a problem rather than hide it until I return

That last one is worth sitting with. If your team would rather absorb a problem than call you about it, that reflects something about how problems have been received in the past, and it is a real risk during any absence.

Reading your score

Fewer than five no answers: you are in good shape. Take the week. The remaining gaps will surface while you are away and you will fix them with real information.

Five to twelve: typical for an owner-operated business. You could probably manage a long weekend now, and a full week after fixing your worst section. Work on the section with the most gaps, not a bit of everything.

More than twelve: the business currently depends on you almost entirely. This is extremely common and it is not a judgment on how you have run things. It does mean that a sudden absence, not a chosen one, would be genuinely serious, and that is worth treating as urgent rather than aspirational.

The order to fix things in

  1. Access first. Fastest, cheapest, and the most damaging to leave broken.
  2. Decisions second. Biggest daily impact on both your time and customer experience.
  3. Knowledge third. Slowest, but compounds, and it is the foundation for everything else.
  4. Money fourth, deliberately, because it needs care rather than speed.

The actual test

Checklists approximate. The real test is taking an ordinary week off, without preparing for it, unreachable except for genuine emergencies. Do not do this if you scored badly. Fix the worst section first, take a long weekend, see what breaks, fix that, then try the week. What breaks is not a failure. It is the most accurate possible diagnosis of your remaining dependencies, generated in days rather than months of thinking about it.

Why this matters beyond the holiday

A business that cannot run without you is a business you cannot sell, cannot scale past your own hours, and cannot step back from if your health or family situation changes. Those are big consequences quietly attached to what looks like a question about taking time off. It is also, for most owners here, the actual point of the whole exercise. You started the business for freedom, and somewhere along the way it became the thing you are least free from.
